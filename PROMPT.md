# Unity 6 build brief: procedural pizza-cart delivery game

Paste this entire brief into the coding agent working with the **existing Unity 6 project and its already-installed, already-running Unity MCP connection**. This document is the source of truth for the project until the human owner explicitly changes it. Do not start building when receiving this document in the current planning conversation. When the owner tells you to begin, inspect the project and proceed in the phases below. Do not publish to itch.io until the owner has played and approved the result.

## Role and priorities

You are the game's designer, gameplay engineer, technical artist, procedural-generation engineer, and ruthless playtest critic. Your job is to make a short browser game that feels immediately wonderful to steer and looks intentionally art-directed. The priority order is:

1. Tactile cart control, satisfying momentum, genuinely absurd and funny recoverable physical accidents, and a legible camera.
2. A coherent, distinctive, non-realistic 3D art direction that actually runs well in a browser.
3. Procedural generation of meaningful 2–3 minute delivery challenges, not cosmetic randomized scenery.
4. A clear objective and very simple visual communication.
5. Breadth of biomes, props, features, menus, and polish that do not improve the above.

If time is tight, cut breadth. Do not cut the core feel or PCG validation. One memorable environment with excellent movement beats is better than three generic, under-finished asset packs. Do not quietly substitute a template runner, fixed three-lane course, stock vehicle controller, or repetitive random obstacle spawner.

## Elevator pitch and player goal

A pizza courier, seen from behind, rides **inside the basket of a shopping cart**. He grips the cart with his left hand and balances a plain closed pizza box **on the upturned fingertips of his right hand, underneath the center of the box's base**. He does not palm it, grip an edge, touch the lid, or lift it high overhead. His right elbow is bent, and the box sits roughly around shoulder height, flat and clearly silhouetted. The cart tears through a comically over-engineered neighborhood, taking ramps, drifts, moving surfaces, bumps, and shortcuts. The guy and pizza react separately to every impact. He is not a polished superhero; his desperation to keep one ordinary pizza level while everything else goes wrong is the joke.

The objective of a run is simple: **deliver the pizza to a visible customer destination before the timer expires, with the pizza still deliverable.** Each delivery takes about two to three minutes for a first successful attempt. A retry produces a new route from a new seed; a seed can be replayed for fair comparison. End with a short results beat showing delivery time and pizza condition, and a single obvious replay action. A score or tip can exist as a secondary reward, never as the only stated goal. Avoid a complex economy, quests, lore dumps, dialogue, collectible spam, and an endless treadmill. This is a finite, replayable run with a clear finish.

The main loop: see the destination direction, commit to a route, steer and drift through physical terrain, decide whether to take a safe detour or high-speed trick route, keep the pizza stable, recover from hilarious impacts, deliver, retry. A failed trick should normally cost time, heat, or pizza condition rather than cause an instant death. The player should be laughing and still have a chance to save the run.

## Nonnegotiable visual target

Use the three supplied gameplay concept images as **composition and interaction references only**, not production asset specifications. They show the camera behind and above the cart, the rider centered low, a clearly visible forward route, and alternate paths. They still lean too glossy and too detailed in places. The actual game should be more deliberately stylized, with coherent shape language and readable space. If the images are not attached to the build session, use this written specification; never claim you saw a missing reference.

Target a polished, immersive, mid-polygon-to-high-quality stylized 3D scene built from deliberately shaped meshes. Human proportions can be gently caricatured, not grotesque. The cart silhouette and four wheels must read instantly. Buildings and terrain use warm, slightly imperfect, handmade forms, believable architectural rhythm, simplified surface detail, and selective hand-painted texture accents. A subtle oil-on-wood or painted-material sensibility is welcome, but this must remain spatially clear 3D gameplay, not a filter that makes hazards hard to parse. Favor matte materials, controlled roughness, warm bounce light, restrained stylized shadows, a strong near/mid/far depth hierarchy, and precise color separation between traversable ground, moving surfaces, hazards, and background. Do not imitate a named existing game's exact visual assets.

The initial environment should be one coherent but delightfully nonsensical delivery district. A hilly town, compact market, canal/park edge, and neighborhood finish are possible spatial ingredients, not a mandate for a quaint realistic town. Give ordinary places an absurd playable twist: a market's runaway checkout conveyor spans a canal; a delivery ramp is an oversized folding table; a sprinkler turns a shortcut into a sudden drift surface. These things must make visual and physical sense once seen, even though no sane city planner would have built them. Distinct **functional zones** may appear within it, but they must feel geographically connected. No need for three disconnected forest/mall/factory packs in the first playable version. A setting selector is optional later; each selected setting must still generate varied gameplay, rather than select one fixed level. The route is free-steering within broad traversable ribbons and junction spaces. It is not three rigid lanes. Yet it is also not an enormous empty open world where players lose the destination.

The courier is a white, blond-haired young man seen from the back. He wears tan/neutral shorts, a red cap with blond hair visible below the back, and a red T-shirt with black half sleeves. The pizza box is plain and **not a speaking character**. Do not give pizza slices, burgers, or other food mascots faces, limbs, or personalities. A very selective non-food object may have an expressive face or behavior if it creates a genuinely good, functional gag, such as a grumpy gate that slams shut, but it is not required and must never replace a readable mechanic. Limit any such character to a memorable authored encounter, not a world full of talking props. Humor comes primarily from motion, animation, staging, reactions, sound, timing, and the absurd delivery situation. If a prop needs to explain its function, solve it through geometry, animation, color, and affordance first. Do not create slick photorealistic surfaces, stock-food mascots, plastic toy-world rendering, bloom fog, endless confetti, floating text, or generic AI-looking scenery.

Composition requirements: a springy chase camera follows a few meters behind and a little above. Keep cart, hand, and pizza readable without occluding approaching geometry. Show enough horizon to plan turns and jumps. Avoid extreme FOV distortion, aggressive camera shake, excessive speed lines, motion blur, intrusive DOF, and the cart covering the route. At forks, frame both options early enough to choose. Distinguish each route by silhouette and material before color, and ensure hazard legibility in grayscale and at a small itch embed size. Use attractive lighting without sacrificing legibility. Take actual Unity screenshots at gameplay resolution and compare to this brief; concept art is not proof that the game looks good in-engine.

## Game-feel contract, before procedural breadth

Build and tune a **20–30 second hand-authored feel playground first**. It needs a straight for acceleration, a wide corner, one bump, a controllable drift, a small ramp and generous landing, one moving surface, one harmless wall collision, and a short finish. The player must get to the satisfying part within seconds. Do not build the full generator until this playground is genuinely fun on keyboard and in a browser build.

Control proposal, subject to playtesting: constant forward drive with analog-feeling left/right steering on A/D or arrows, a forgiving hop on Space, and a single hold-to-drift/brake control such as Shift. A separate brief hold-to-steady pizza action may be tried only if it materially improves the playtest. Do not force a two-axis hand-balancing minigame, mouse-and-keyboard gymnastics, or six simultaneous controls just because the premise permits it. The pizza can physically wobble from acceleration and impacts through an authored spring/animation response; the player's steering, route choice, hop timing, and optional steady action can influence its stability. Keep the box supported visually by the fingertips even as it tilts and rebounds. Do not make the player fight random unstable physics every second.

Implement deterministic, tunable motion rather than letting uncontrolled rigidbody collisions author the game. A kinematic or carefully constrained dynamic cart controller is acceptable. Whatever is chosen, the cart must retain enough predictable momentum to learn. Steering should turn quickly enough for visible route decisions, but not snap at speed. Drift should widen the arc, increase yaw/slip, and make a rewarding skid before a clean regrip. Jumps need coyote/grace timing if helpful, legible launch impulse, a forgiving landing window, wheel compression, rider lag, pizza lag, and a quick recovery. There must be no surprise catapulting, jitter, wall-clipping, or camera clipping. On collision, let the cart scrape, wobble and lose speed, then recover, rather than abruptly stop or explode. Have a reset-to-track safeguard if a cart becomes unrecoverably stuck.

Feedback should be layered and causally tied to the simulation: wheel caster spin, cart frame rattle, tire/skid sound, brief suspension compression, rider torso follow-through, elbow correction, pizza box tilt, a small landing squash, subtle camera lag, and a restrained impact sound. Use screen shake only for a genuinely large hit, in a short bounded burst. Dust/splashes are short and directional, not particle clutter. On a clean drift or jump, reward timing and control with sound, motion, and a clear gain in speed or route position. Prefer simple recorded or properly licensed audio and authored animation curves to generic stock stingers, quippy voice lines, and gratuitous VFX. Audio should respect browser activation and mute controls.

Define baseline target numbers in config as **hypotheses, not truths**: e.g. ~0.15–0.25 seconds from input to visible steering response, modest hop airtime that permits a readable landing, a 3–6 second breather after a major stunt, and a target 60 fps on an ordinary desktop browser. Playtest and adjust rather than presenting these as proven universal constants. Test responsiveness at 30 fps too. Log the feel tweaks and test result in PROGRESSION.md. Never use random impulses for primary control feel.

## The PCG method: intent first, geometry second

Implement a **seeded, hierarchical, gameplay-grammar generator with constraint checking and generate-test-repair**. The word grammar here means authored rules over gameplay beats and route graph structure, not decorative L-systems for trees. L-systems can later dress vegetation but are irrelevant to core PCG. Wave Function Collapse is not required and does not itself guarantee a completable, well-paced delivery. A heavy general-purpose CSP/ASP solver is not required for this prototype; explicit typed constraints and bounded backtracking can be simpler, inspectable, and sufficient. If a solver is considered, justify it by a demonstrated need and Web build cost.

Generation stages, preferably separated into testable data structures before GameObject instantiation:

1. **Mission contract.** Choose a seed, destination, biome palette, target travel-time band, difficulty, and a small list of intended player actions for this delivery. Pick a soft risk/reward premise, such as a fast bumpy shortcut against a safer warm route. Avoid making pizza temperature a complex second timer unless testing proves it interesting. The primary time deadline and integrity are enough to start.
2. **Pacing/action grammar.** Make a macro beat sequence: safe start and visual teaching, build speed, one simple action, recovery, meaningful fork, a signature trick or slapstick event, recovery, a harder combination, then a readable final sprint and finish. Vary order and instances while constraining repetition. Make a beat sequence that a player can learn and master, not noise for the sake of uniqueness.
3. **Route graph.** Expand beats into a directed graph with a guaranteed primary path plus occasional optional shortcut branches and reunions. At each fork, both choices must connect forward. A branch must change a real gameplay cost, affordance, or outcome. Avoid dead ends in a speed game unless they are clearly telegraphed and recoverable.
4. **Spatial realization.** Fit authored, parameterized 3D gameplay modules to graph nodes and edges. Each prefab has entry/exit sockets with position, heading, width, height, speed envelope, physical surface, clearance envelope, action tag, and collision/safety metadata. Transform and connect them within curvature, slope, clearance, and landing constraints. A few high-quality modules may support controlled variations in length, bank angle, surface, prop placement, and dressing. Never distort geometry beyond tested bounds.
5. **Interaction population.** Place only functional things that act on motion or route choice: bump, springy awning, rolling conveyor, slick spill, timed gate, ramp, curb, reversible wall bump, moving market cart. Every object has a taught behavior and a clear visual/audio cue. Decorative props occupy protected sockets outside navigation and camera sight lines.
6. **Validation and repair.** Test graph connectivity, spatial connectivity, overlap, height and curvature, jump launch-to-landing reach, crossing clearance, player-width margin, response time/preview distance, safe route existence, and pizza-condition budget. Sample intended/poor inputs through a lightweight movement envelope or replayable controller simulation where practicable; graph reachability alone is not proof of physical playability. Reject, backtrack, or repair a bad segment. Use bounded attempts with a safe known-good fallback seed/layout, not an infinite loop or a last-second impossible branch.
7. **Instancing.** Only after the abstract result passes checks, spawn/pool modules and props, bake or configure static data as needed, and start the run. Prefer generating the whole finite map before play, or enough of it to avoid visible hitches. Keep seeded generation deterministic. Separate gameplay RNG from cosmetic RNG so changing a shrub does not change the route.

Example data, adapt to the project rather than copying blindly:

```
Beat: Drift | Hop | MovingSurface | Recover | Fork | Finish
Module: id, biome, prefab, entrySockets[], exitSockets[],
        width, lengthRange, curvatureRange, slopeRange,
        approachSpeedRange, exitSpeedRange, clearanceBounds,
        actionTags[], riskCost, recoveryDemand, previewDistance,
        propSockets[], cameraOcclusionBounds, testedVariants[]
Route: seed, beatGraph, spatialModules, protectedSafeCorridor,
       optionalShortcuts, checkpoints, expectedTravelTimeBand
```

Build a legible, versioned library of hand-authored modules. A module is not a fixed full level: it is a reusable geometric/mechanical vocabulary with parameters and multiple compatible joins. The generator combines modules into new topologies, rhythms, shortcut choices, and hazards. Compare several seeds by **action trajectory** and branching, not just screenshots with different props. A run must meaningfully change a player's sequence of steering, hopping, drifting, recovering, and choosing. Keep a debug readout in editor only with seed, graph, chosen modules, rejected attempts/reasons, estimated time, safe-route existence, action sequence, and generation time. Offer a copy-seed feature on results/debug screens, not text clutter in play.

Hard invariants: a path from start to delivery exists; no physical seams or floating modules; every mandatory jump has a reachable landing for the tested speed envelope; camera can show required decisions before commitment; no two high-punishment beats directly adjacent without recovery; at least one path supports delivery within the timer and condition threshold for a competent but non-perfect run; optional risky paths give an actual payoff; both branches of a fork are distinct and valid; no hazards spawn in the protected safe corridor unless they are explicitly designed to be passable. Do not claim 'error-free' as a mathematical guarantee if the checks do not prove it. State exactly what is formally checked and what needs empirical human playtesting.

Soft quality goals: no duplicate signature stunt in adjacent sections; useful variety in action tags and geometry; 1–2 visible choices in a two-minute course, not a fork every ten seconds; sightlines at decision points; short calm intervals; escalating but bounded difficulty; a memorable accident or recovery opportunity; visual continuity of destination. Reject runs that are technically valid but flat, overly repetitive, too hard, or too long. Start with authored weight rules and simple scoring; tune from seed tests and playtests. Resist procedural novelty at the cost of fun.

Create tests for at least hundreds of seeds in editor without rendering, then physically play representative seeds including easiest, hardest, weirdest, most repetitive, and every generator fallback. Track failure rate by reason, generation time, map duration, branch frequency, action-sequence diversity, safe-route success, collision hotspots, and pizza-delivery success. Use a debug free camera and graph view to inspect bad seeds. Keep a curated regression set of bad seeds and do not remove a constraint merely to make a failing seed pass. Have a deterministic golden-seed smoke test. Automated traversal is a useful filter, not a substitute for humans playing at speed.

## Interaction grammar and humor

The desired tone is **physical nonsense with understandable rules**. The premise itself should make someone laugh before they press Play: a guy is delivering a single pizza while seated in a shopping cart, and the world is inexplicably structured to make that task ridiculous. Each run should contain at least one absurd situation that could be described as an action, not merely an odd-looking object. Examples: a giant rotating shop sign briefly becomes a launch platform; an automated car wash accidentally cleans the cart but soaks the pizza box; a folding market stall flips open under the wheels and becomes a ramp; a conveyor takes the cart through a shortcut and ejects it sideways into a perfect landing. These are candidate interactions, not a shopping list. Pick only those that are feasible, legible at speed, and genuinely fun in play. The player should be able to say, 'I cannot believe that worked,' then deliberately try it again on the next seed.

Avoid safe, sanitized whimsy, random meme jokes, joke signs, puns on every surface, and incoherent asset mashups. Surprise should come from a clear setup, a physical payoff, and a short recovery. The route generator should assemble these setups in different contexts, with rules that prevent a gag from breaking playability or repeating until it stops being funny. Run a simple humor test in playtests: ask what moment made the player laugh or tell someone else about the run. If nobody recalls a moment, improve the interaction and timing rather than writing more jokes.

The nouns should not all be 'things that kill you.' Every biome-like district needs a function and a distinct physical joke:

- Hilly town: long sightlines, driftable switchbacks, low curb hops, downhill speed.
- Market: narrow but fair navigation, sliding produce spills, wobbling canopy or crate shortcuts, recoverable near misses.
- Canal/park: a conveyor or moving boardwalk and a slower safe route, water that slows or reroutes rather than instantly kills.
- Neighborhood finish: a visible destination, one satisfying combination of mastered moves, and a final landing/recovery.

These are examples, not a mandatory asset shopping list. First make two or three beautifully authored interaction archetypes and combine them in varied routes. Funny effects must emerge from cause and consequence: a low bump makes the courier momentarily lift while the pizza lags; the box settles on his fingertips; an awkward drift saves it; the cart rattles through a shortcut and reaches the door by a hair. Do not add jokey on-screen captions or mascot props to tell the player something is funny.

Pizza condition should be an understandable, forgiving consequence system. Start with one integrity meter expressed mostly in the physical box: slight wobble, greasy/creased box, lid ajar, then a visibly ruined delivery. If heat becomes a meaningful route tradeoff after playtests, add it sparingly, with unmistakable cues like steam and a warm/cool route, without two near-identical bars. Do not punish every little bump. A player should have a visible 'oh no' moment and a chance to correct before losing. Make failures dramatic but brief, with instant retry. If a pizza is ruined before the end, allow a quick recoverable setback or restart, not two minutes of doomed driving.

## UI, language, tutorial, accessibility

Extremely low text on screen. A seven-year-old who reads basic English should understand the goal in a glance. Opening UI may say 'Deliver the pizza!' with a destination icon and one obvious Play button. During play show only what is essential: a big visual destination cue, a simple time indicator, and a pizza-condition cue. Avoid paragraphs, lore, currency screens, long stat labels, multiple minimaps, popping score text, floating damage numbers, corporate/marketing phrasing, and AI-sounding quips. Copy must be short, natural, funny only where it earns a laugh. No em dashes in game text. Keep all text editable and run spelling checks.

Teach by level design: first safe curve, then one slow bump, then a clear ramp and landing, then a fork where safe/fast geometry looks different. For an input the player genuinely cannot infer, use a **brief game-pausing overlay** that darkens the scene, highlights the actual relevant surface or cart feature, shows one input icon and at most a few plain words, then lets the player try it immediately in a non-punishing situation. Overlay should not cover the upcoming landing, must be dismissible, and should appear only once per mechanic unless tutorial replay is selected. Pause timer and physics cleanly. Never put a wall of directions at launch. Add color-blind-friendly shape/material/audio redundancy; subtitle any required speech, but speech is not required. Provide mute, pause, restart, and sensible browser focus behavior.

The end goal, **after the owner is satisfied**, is a click-to-play Unity Web build embedded on itch.io. Do not publish, create a public page, or upload a build just because the game compiles. Prepare a Web build profile early and test on a local HTTP server, then in the real itch embed before calling it done. Never test Unity Web output by opening index.html as a file URL. Keep current itch HTML-game requirements in mind: a ZIP with `index.html` at its root and relative asset paths; fewer than 1,000 extracted files; at most 500 MB extracted total and 200 MB for any single extracted file; filenames within 240 characters and case-correct. A fallback can simplify hosting but increase loader and startup cost. Check the Unity Web build after every significant rendering, input, audio, or asset-pipeline change. Make a reproducible packaging checklist, verify ZIP structure and extracted sizes, and test the uploaded private/draft page. Publish only after explicit owner approval.

## Research basis and verification notes

This design is informed by primary sources, but the exact controls, numbers, and art direction above are project hypotheses to be tested, not findings guaranteed by the papers:

- Linden, Lopes and Bidarra, *Designing Procedurally Generated Levels* (AIIDE 2013): designer constraints and graph grammars can produce action sequences and associated level content. https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIIDE/article/view/12592
- Smith, Whitehead and Mateas, *Tanagra* (AIIDE 2010): planning/constraint programming tied to movement and geometric reachability. Our 3D cart game must implement and test its own movement envelope. https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIIDE/article/view/12379
- Horswill and Foged, *Fast Procedural Level Population with Playability Constraints* (AIIDE 2012): path-level conditions matter beyond local placement. https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIIDE/article/view/12511
- Beukman, James and Cleghorn, *Towards Objective Metrics for Procedurally Generated Video Game Levels* (2022): action-trajectory diversity is more meaningful than surface differences alone, while metrics have limitations. https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.10334
- Mark Johnson, GDC, *Handmade Detail in a Procedural World*: avoid arbitrary procedural additions disconnected from design goals. https://gdcvault.com/play/1022837/Handmade-Detail-in-a-Procedural
- Celia Hodent, *The Gamer's Brain, Part 2*: attention, perceivable cues and active, non-punishing onboarding. https://celiahodent.com/gamers-brain-ux-onboarding/
- Unity 6 Web deployment and performance documentation. Recheck the installed editor's exact version: https://docs.unity.com/en-us/engine/6000.0/manual/platform-specific/webgl/building-distribution/deploying and https://docs.unity.com/en-us/engine/6000.6/manual/platform-specific/webgl/develop/performance
- itch.io official HTML5 uploading requirements, including current ZIP constraints and embed behavior: https://itch.io/docs/creators/html5

End of brief. At kickoff, report Phase 0 findings, create the two root documents, and show the first small visual/feel experiment. Do not claim the game is built or published until it actually is.
